Preface

My grandmother Zina used to say:

“Life is no field to cross.”

I think that is what each of our journeys looks like. Sometimes the path stretches out before us, wide and open. Sometimes it becomes so difficult that we cannot see where to take the next step.

Dear Grandma, I love you so much. And I know you are always near. Just as those who are no longer physically here are near, too, their love living within me every day.

I have had to live through things most people would rather not speak about. And I know the value of silence. But I also know how important it is, in the hardest moments, to have someone beside you who is willing to share that silence. Someone who does not run away, look aside, or rush to find the right words. Someone who can listen to your thoughts, share moments of sadness, and simply stay, without trying to “fix” the pain or make it smaller.

I am writing this book for those who need someone like that beside them right now.

This is a book about love.

About the love we search for, sometimes without knowing how to notice that it is already there. About the love we try to hold on to because we are afraid of losing it. About love for our children, which changes how we understand ourselves and the world. About a love that does not disappear, even when everything that once made up our lives changes.

This book holds my motherhood, my relationships, encounters and partings. It holds joy, exhaustion, mistakes, and discoveries that took me years to reach.

And it holds a loss that is still difficult for me to speak about.

I have worked as a psychologist for many years. But when it came to my own life, I discovered that knowledge and experience do not provide answers to the questions that matter most. I did not know the right way to live through loss, how much time it would take to feel joy again, or whether there is any right way at all to live through what happens to us.

I do not want to turn what I have lived through into a set of neat conclusions.

I want to tell my story. Honestly. Without hiding its contradictions, without trying to make myself seem better than I was, and without insisting that everything that happened must have a meaning.

Perhaps someone will recognize themselves in this story. Perhaps it will help someone feel that, even in our most difficult experiences, we are not always as alone as we might think.

I want there to be room here for everything: for love and anger, tenderness and disappointment, gratitude and regret. For questions that do not yet have answers.

I am also writing for those who are no longer physically beside me, but whose love has stayed with me.

For my grandmother.

And above all, for my Marusya.

This book is my conversation with her, across space and time.

I do not know what lies beyond the limits of our earthly experience. But I know that the love I feel for my daughter did not end when I could no longer hold her, hear her voice, or watch her grow.

It continues to live within me, changing me, my relationships with others, and the way I see the world.

And perhaps that is why, one day, I was able to begin writing.

Chapter 1. Searching for Love

I think most of us feel there is never quite enough love. At least, for a long time, I never felt I had enough.

I looked for it in people, in relationships, in the way others looked at me. I wanted to be chosen. I wanted someone's presence to reassure me that I was all right, that I could be loved and, above all, that I would not be left.

I grew up in a good family. It matters to me to say that from the start. My childhood fell in the 1990s, when adults had to work hard, find the things their families needed, and make their way through ordinary but difficult circumstances. Love was often expressed not in conversations about feelings but in acts of care: food on the table, warm clothes, going to work even when there was no strength left.

Today I can recognize the love in all of that. But as a child, I could not always feel it.

I was loved. And I did not feel loved enough.

One does not cancel out the other.

A child does not know how much responsibility adults carry. A child simply wants to be noticed. Not for good grades, not for being obedient, not for being easy to look after. Just for being there. To be asked not only how the day went, but how it felt.

I think that was what I sometimes missed: not love itself, but the chance to feel it.

And gradually I began looking outside myself for proof that I mattered. I hoped that one day someone would come along whose presence would put an end to my doubts about myself.

I did not simply want to love. I wanted someone to make up for all the love I felt I had missed.

I married at twenty-one. Back then, I considered myself an adult and knew exactly what I wanted: a family, a home of my own, closeness. Someone beside whom I could finally stop searching.

Sasha came into my life and seemed to wrap me in a warm blanket.

Before him, I often felt that love had to be earned. That I had to ask for it in tiny portions, guess another person's moods, try to please them, be interesting, needed, special.

But Sasha simply loved me.

Without complicated conditions. Without making me prove, again and again, that I deserved his attention.

At first, I was rather bold with him. I kept threatening to leave. I thought this was not quite what I wanted. That somewhere there must be another kind of love: more exalted, extraordinary, the kind that takes your breath away.

And he kept courting me.

Again and again and again.

Until, at some point, I gave in.

There was something almost parental about the way he cared for me. Not because I saw him as a father. It was simply that, with him, I could let my guard down.

He solved problems before they even had a chance to become problems.

I remember once scraping someone else's car in a parking lot. I called Sasha, and within literally five minutes he was there. I did not have to explain anything or work out how to deal with the situation. He came and took care of it.

That was how things were almost all the time.

With him, I hardly worried about anything.

It was as though someone had put warm socks on my feet, made me a cup of tea, tucked me into a blanket, and said:

“Just sit there. I'll take care of everything.”

Sasha was a straightforward man. He did not try to turn our life into an endless romantic story.

But that was exactly the kind of story I wanted.

I dreamed of something more, without always understanding what I meant by “more.”

I remember sitting in the kitchen eating dried, salted fish. Sasha watched me with such tenderness that I can still remember his expression all these years later.

I was twenty-one.

I do not know what our separation cost him. And I do not want to invent his feelings in hindsight.

But now, remembering that kitchen and that look, I understand that the man beside me knew how to love me in a wonderfully simple way.

Sometimes I want to go back to that twenty-one-year-old woman. Not to warn her about every mistake or tell her how her first marriage will end. Just to sit beside her.

I do not want to judge her. She was looking for love, and she loved as best she knew how. Perhaps part of growing up is learning to acknowledge our mistakes without deciding that we ourselves were a mistake.

Now I understand that Sasha's care was real. And my longing for a different kind of love was real, too. I do not need to dismiss either his feelings or my own to explain why we did not stay together.

For a long time I thought happiness meant finding someone who would never leave. Gradually I began to understand that even the greatest love another person can give cannot replace a relationship with oneself.

Lesson in Love No. 1. Another Person's Love Is Not Enough

There was a time when I believed that if someone loved me strongly enough, all my doubts about my own worth would finally disappear.

I have been chosen, so I matter.

I am loved, so there is nothing wrong with me.

I have not been left, so I can feel safe.

But little by little I began to see how fragile that kind of foundation was.

Another person's love is a beautiful thing. We need other people. We need embraces, closeness, presence, words, touch.

I have never felt comfortable with the idea that all a person has to do is “love themselves” and they will never need anyone else.

Of course they will.

We are made for relationships.

I think one of our greatest mistakes is confusing inner steadiness with independence from other people. As though growing up meant learning not to need anyone at all.

But I do not want a life in which I need no one.

I want to love. I want to be loved. I want to miss someone, look forward to seeing them, feel close, share my life with another person.

And, at the same time, learn not to lose myself in that love.

It took me a long time to begin distinguishing two things: the need for closeness, and the expectation that another person will somehow fill every space left empty by what I did not receive in the past.

No one can go back and love us enough to make up for our entire past. But that does not make other people's love any less necessary.

Perhaps, for me, growing up in love does not mean ceasing to need others.

It means one day being able to say:

I choose you not because I cease to exist without you.

I choose you because I do exist.

And I want to be beside you.

I did not see that distinction straight away. And perhaps I am still learning.

Today I want to thank the person with whom my family life began.

Thank you for the love you gave me.

For the time we spent together.

For the good that existed between us.

I am sorry that back then there were things I did not understand and things I did not know how to do. That sometimes I expected you to answer questions I could only ask myself.

I do not want to rewrite our story and pretend that everything in it was wonderful. But neither do I want its ending to strip away the meaning of what was genuine between us.

You were not my mistake.

We were two people who once chose each other, and later could not sustain that choice for a lifetime.

And today I can look at that without needing to blame anyone.

I am grateful that we met.

And that, years later, I can look back at my younger self with greater tenderness.

But another kind of love lay ahead of me. One I felt before I even tried to understand it.

A love that came not through promises or certainty that I was making the right choice, but through a strange, almost inexplicable recognition in my body.

My mind was still asking questions. My body had already answered.
